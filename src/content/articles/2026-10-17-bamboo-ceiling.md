I'm a software engineer from Taiwan. I joined my current team in April, and six months later
the hardest problem I face at work isn't technical. It's communication. I'm the only
first-generation immigrant on the team and the only non-native English speaker. My ear isn't
trained yet for the accents around me, and my teammates find my accent just as hard.
Communication runs both ways, but when one person is the odd one out, every gap looks like it
starts with that person.

In US workforce data, I share one label with millions of engineers: "Asian." Our paths to
leadership don't look alike. Here's what the research says, and what I'm doing about it.

## Why this ceiling stays invisible

US workforce data usually has one "Asian" category. When Lu reviewed the S&P 100 in 2024, every
company's diversity reporting lumped Asian subgroups together [[2]](#ref-2). That box hides the
pattern.

Lu, Nisbett, and Morris split it. Across nine studies with 11,030 people, East Asians were less
likely than both South Asians and White Americans to reach leadership positions, while South
Asians were more likely than White Americans to do so [[1]](#ref-1). Per million people in the
US, S&P 500 companies from 2010 to 2017 had 2.82 South Asian CEOs, 1.92 White CEOs, and 0.59
East Asian CEOs.

For an individual contributor (IC), the ceiling shows up at promotion, where influence starts to count. An Ascend Foundation analysis of national EEOC data found that Asian
Americans as a whole are the least likely group to be promoted from individual contributor roles
into management [[3]](#ref-3). A manager who only sees the "Asian" average won't notice which engineers are stalling, or why senior talent leaves.

## What the ceiling is made of

Start with what it isn't. Across their studies, Lu and colleagues controlled for factors such as
education, socioeconomic status, US birth, and English fluency, and the gap remained. East and South Asians didn't differ in
work or leadership motivation. South Asians reported *more* prejudice than East Asians, not
less, and still reached leadership more often [[1]](#ref-1). So the gap isn't about ability, and
no group is holding another back.

What best explained it was assertiveness, which accounted for part of the gap in every study that
tested it. US workplaces read speaking up as leadership; East Asian norms prize restraint
[[1]](#ref-1). I grew up with a simple rule: be humble, care about quality,
talk less, do more. In Taiwan it served me well. In a US meeting, quiet quality reads as having
nothing to say. Garry Tan, now CEO of Y Combinator, says the habit of keeping his head down and
waiting to be noticed cost him about ten years of his career [[4]](#ref-4).

First-generation immigrants face a second obstacle: language. One 2024 reanalysis of Lu's data
suggests the ceiling falls hardest on foreign-born East Asians [[5]](#ref-5), though other work
finds it in both generations [[7]](#ref-7). Language doesn't explain the group gap, but it makes assertiveness harder. In a fast discussion, I can't always process what I hear in real time. And listeners rate statements as less credible
when the speaker has a heavy accent [[6]](#ref-6). Across 15 multinational teams, colleagues read weaker fluency as weaker competence, which slowed trust
[[8]](#ref-8).

Lu also found that East Asians stick with their own group more than others do. In his MBA
classes, students with fewer friends outside their group were less often chosen as leaders
[[7]](#ref-7). The flip side is a lever: ties across groups. That's one thing I can change.

Here's the mechanism. Promotion decisions run on stories other people can retell: who shaped the
design, who unblocked the launch, whose judgment the team leaned on. A culture of restraint means
I don't claim that story. A language gap means I tell it late. The work is real, but the
narrative goes to whoever speaks first. That's the ceiling: not weaker work, but impact with no
one carrying it into the room.

Where work is judged on paper, the picture flips. A MacroPolo analysis of NeurIPS 2022 authors
found that 47% of top-tier AI researchers did their undergraduate degree in China
[[9]](#ref-9). Papers don't have accents.

## How I'm working on it

Our company has an Asian employee group, and I'm glad it exists. But belonging to a category
isn't the same as having someone who shares your problem. So I stopped waiting for the right
group to appear and started building those connections myself. Tan's framing helps: culture
shapes you whether or not you notice it, and once you notice it, you can choose differently
[[4]](#ref-4).

- **Cross the circle.** I'm setting up a one-on-one with every teammate. One-on-ones are the lowest-friction place to talk: one accent at a time, at a
  pace we can both follow.
- **Write first, speak second.** Design docs, short proposals, and a word with key people before meetings. In writing, accent and speed disappear, and the idea gets judged
  on its own. In the meeting, I make one prepared point, early.
- **Train my ear on purpose.** Native English listeners who trained on several speakers of one
  foreign accent got better at understanding a new speaker of that accent [[10]](#ref-10). I'm betting it works for me too, so I treat talks in those accents as practice, not background noise.
- **Propose two norms, then use them.** Every decision lands in the team channel with an owner
  and a date; an AI meeting summary makes that nearly free. Every project gets a named owner, and
  I ask to own one end to end.

Tan's own answer to the ceiling was to leave and build his own house [[4]](#ref-4). I don't
know yet whether that's mine. I'm starting with the parts I control.

## Takeaways

- "Asian" hides the bamboo ceiling. Break promotion data into East, South, and Southeast Asian,
  so the engineers who stall don't disappear into an average.
- For East Asians, the research points to assertiveness, not ability or prejudice. For
  first-generation immigrants, language is a second obstacle.
- Make impact visible: write it down, build one-on-one trust, and ask for visible ownership.

## Question for you

Does your company break its "Asian" promotion data into East, South, and Southeast Asian? If
not, who might be stalling without anyone noticing?
